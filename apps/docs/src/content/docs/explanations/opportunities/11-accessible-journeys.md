---
title: Helping passengers avoid inaccessible transfers
description: A planner can check surveyed transfer paths against access needs while clearly separating unconfirmed vehicle boarding conditions.
---

A journey can look convenient on a timetable and still be impossible for the passenger taking it. Consider an illustrative LionLink passenger who uses a wheelchair and wants to transfer from a bus to a train. The bus stop is close to the station, but the shortest entrance has stairs. The other entrance has a lift whose availability has not been checked. A journey planner that returns the fastest connection has answered the wrong question. The passenger needs to know whether they can complete every part of the journey, including the movement between vehicles.

The consequence extends beyond an inconvenient detour. An unusable transfer can leave the passenger seeking assistance after the bus has departed, with no clear alternative. LionLink staff may then have to reconstruct the passenger's location and options under time pressure. These are plausible consequences, not measured outcomes in the supplied records. The [selected passenger accounts](/docs/passenger-findings/) cannot establish how often inaccessible transfers occur or how many passengers would use this service. Before funding a substantial build, LionLink needs to observe journey planning with passengers who have different access needs and establish which information they cannot obtain today.

The repository provides a starting point for describing the network. Its operating records contain stop coordinates and the order of stops along a route. They also describe rail connections and vehicle wheelchair spaces. However, a line between two coordinates does not prove that someone can walk or wheel between them. The rail connections are schematic, and vehicle capacity does not establish that a space will be available when the passenger boards. The [evidence sources](/docs/applications/) explain the fictional and bounded nature of these records. A working service requires new, maintained evidence about the physical journey, rather than a more attractive presentation of the existing map.

The proposed system represents a journey as a sequence of specific connections. A connection might mean moving from a bus boarding point to a crossing, passing through a station entrance, or riding between stops. Engineers call this representation a graph. Its points identify places, and its links describe permitted movements between those places. Each link records the access conditions that matter, such as stairs, gradient, distance, and lift dependence. It also records who verified those conditions and when the evidence needs checking again. A passenger selects requirements, and the search excludes connections that cannot satisfy them before it compares journey times.

For the illustrative transfer, suppose a surveyed route through the main entrance has stairs while a longer route through the north entrance has a verified working lift. A passenger who requires step-free travel receives the north entrance route, with instructions that identify the entrance rather than merely naming the station. If a current closure report then marks that lift unavailable, the planner removes that connection and searches again. If the only remaining alternative has never been surveyed, it reports that no verified journey is available. It explains the unresolved connection and offers the operator's established assistance channel. It does not convert missing evidence into an assurance or quietly relax the passenger's requirements.

That treatment of uncertainty is the principal reason to build this system. A general map search can find nearby stops, but proximity alone cannot enforce access requirements across a complete journey. A well-maintained printed guide may be sufficient for a small, stable interchange. Telephone assistance may also be the better investment if demand is low and staff already have dependable access information. Software becomes more useful when passengers need to compare several journeys and closures change which combinations remain usable. The business case depends on repeated decisions that maintained connection data can improve, not on the existence of an algorithm.

A sensible first deliverable covers surveyed transfers between two bus routes and one rail interchange. It would be labelled transfer-access support, because it cannot yet verify every boarding condition. The result must say that vehicle boarding suitability remains unconfirmed alongside any unresolved space availability. Claiming a fully accessible journey would also require the assigned vehicle, working ramp or lift, boarding-point compatibility, and an owner who keeps that information current. LionLink's accessibility lead and station partner would agree which requirements the pilot supports. Surveyors would record the actual transfer paths, and an identified infrastructure owner would accept responsibility for each update. The engineering team would build the constrained search and a passenger-facing result that distinguishes verified access, known barriers, and unknown conditions. Route and stop identifiers would connect the survey to LionLink's network records. Lift availability would need an agreed update feed or a dependable manual process with expiry times. Without that ownership, the planner would become less trustworthy as soon as infrastructure changed.

The pilot should let passengers search without creating an account. Access requirements can reveal sensitive personal needs, so preferences should remain on the device where practical. Staff need a way to withdraw an incorrect connection immediately and see which published journeys depend on it. When a required update is overdue, the system should stop treating the connection as verified. A service outage should leave users with a clearly dated route guide and the existing assistance process. The first release should make no promise about available wheelchair spaces or assistance reservations unless separate integrations can actually confirm them.

Adoption requires more than publishing the planner. Passengers should test whether the instructions identify the correct boarding point and entrance on location. Customer-service staff should use the same connection descriptions when helping callers. Infrastructure owners need an update routine that fits their existing work, including a named person who handles disputed survey information. Ongoing cost would include resurveying changed paths, responding to reported errors, and checking the accessibility of the planner itself. These duties are central to the proposed service and should appear in its funding decision alongside software development.

A pilot can assess value by comparing the current planning process with the proposed planner on agreed journeys. With appropriate support, participants can attempt planning tasks and explain whether they can identify a usable route and its uncertainties. Reviewers can check every recommended connection against the survey and replay closures to ensure affected routes disappear. Useful measures include incorrect assurances, unresolved planning tasks, time spent seeking assistance, and the effort required to keep information current. None of these measures should treat a confident answer as a success if the underlying route is unusable.

LionLink should proceed with a bounded pilot if passengers identify a material planning problem and infrastructure partners can maintain the required evidence. The next funding decision is therefore whether to support the interchange survey, passenger research, and a narrow working planner together. If verified transfer information cannot be maintained, expanding a digital route search would add confidence without reliability. In that case, improving the published access guide and assistance process is the more defensible investment.
