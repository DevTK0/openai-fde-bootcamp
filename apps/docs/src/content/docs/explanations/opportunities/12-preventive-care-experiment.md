---
title: Finding out whether extra preventive maintenance is worth funding
description: A controlled trial can separate the effect of optional maintenance from changes in vehicle use and ordinary variation.
---

An Engineering manager considering additional condenser cleaning faces a practical funding question. Would paying for more preventive work reduce enough breakdowns or repair unavailability to justify the extra workshop effort? A chart showing lower repair costs after cleaning does not settle the question. Vehicles may have travelled less, a costly repair may have fallen outside the comparison period, or staff may have selected vehicles whose condition was already different. If LionLink expands a programme on that evidence alone, it could buy extra work without knowing whether the work helps.

The supplied fictional records make this question concrete but cannot answer it. They contain monthly kilometres, repairs, additional preventive visits, and repair unavailability for eight selected vehicles. Selected component observations also record condenser obstruction and work categories. The [maintenance findings](/docs/maintenance-findings/) explain the limits of comparisons across this history, while the [cost findings](/docs/cost-findings/) distinguish quoted work from demonstrated savings. The sample is not a complete eligible fleet, and no prospective random assignment connects an intervention to a comparable group. The first task is to establish whether an optional intervention remains uncertain enough to test.

A controlled experiment creates that comparison before outcomes are known. Engineering first identifies vehicles for which either the current maintenance policy or the additional approved cleaning is acceptable. Necessary servicing and corrective repairs continue for every vehicle. Among eligible vehicles, a reproducible random process assigns some to receive the additional intervention and others to continue the existing policy. Random assignment makes it harder for staff expectations or existing condition to determine which group gets the treatment. It does not guarantee that a small trial has perfectly balanced groups or that every difference in results is meaningful.

For an illustrative trial, suppose Engineering considers extra cleaning useful but unproven for a particular vehicle class. The trial register records eligibility, the planned cleaning schedule, and the outcome before making assignments. Vehicles with similar starting use and condition can be grouped before random assignment so that the comparison is less vulnerable to obvious imbalances. If a vehicle assigned cleaning misses its appointment, the register records that missed visit without moving the vehicle into the comparison group. If another vehicle develops a fault, staff repair it according to the normal policy. The experiment never becomes a reason to withhold required work.

At the agreed end of follow-up, the primary analysis compares vehicles according to their original assignments. That comparison answers whether offering the additional programme worked under the conditions of the trial, including missed appointments. Comparing only vehicles that completed every visit could give a misleading answer because those vehicles may differ in other ways. The report should show the estimated difference and the uncertainty around it. A result compatible with both worthwhile benefit and no benefit is inconclusive. The system must be able to present that outcome clearly rather than label whichever group has the lower total as the winner.

Outcome selection needs care. Repair spending is understandable to finance but can be dominated by a rare expensive job. Repair unavailability reflects an operational consequence but requires consistent recording. Distance travelled matters, yet the programme might itself change availability and therefore distance. Dividing by kilometres without deciding how to handle that relationship can answer a different question from the one the sponsor intended. Engineering, operations, finance, and an analyst should agree one primary outcome, the supporting measures, and their interpretation before the trial begins. A sample-size assessment should establish whether the feasible number of vehicles and follow-up period can support a useful decision.

The initial software deliverable can remain small. It needs an eligibility and assignment register, a record of treatment delivery and deviations, an agreed import of outcomes, and a report that preserves the original analysis plan. Each assignment should be reproducible and protected from retrospective editing. A correction should remain visible rather than overwrite the history. The current fictional fields can help design these records, but a live trial needs the complete eligible population, reliable vehicle identifiers, and follow-up beyond a selected historical extract. Integration with workshop bookings can record completed visits without granting the experiment authority to change operational priorities.

The system's main engineering challenge is preserving a defensible comparison through ordinary work. A vehicle might transfer depots, leave the fleet, or lose part of its maintenance history. The register should account for every assigned vehicle and show why follow-up is missing. Staff must be able to report safety concerns and stop an intervention without having to protect the appearance of a successful trial. Engineering owns those decisions. An analyst should examine whether shared workshop capacity or reassigned vehicle work causes the two groups to influence each other. Such effects may require a different trial design rather than another reporting feature.

A spreadsheet and a carefully managed trial protocol may be sufficient for a single small experiment. Custom software is justified if assignments, workshop records, and outcome imports would otherwise be difficult to reconcile, or if LionLink expects to repeat well-governed trials. The proposal should not become a general experiment platform before the first intervention has an approved design. A prototype using invented outcomes can test assignment reproducibility, missing-data handling, and reporting of inconclusive findings. It cannot establish that cleaning works. Operational adoption then depends on workshop staff understanding which visits are additional and on managers accepting that the trial may reject their preferred programme.

The business value comes from improving the investment decision. A credible finding could support wider adoption of worthwhile preventive work or avoid spending on an ineffective programme. Both outcomes can be useful. Trial cost includes the intervention itself, staff coordination, analysis, and the effort of consistent measurement. Before approval, the sponsor should agree what size of improvement would justify those recurring costs and what uncertainty remains acceptable. During the pilot, assess whether assignments remain intact, planned work is delivered, outcomes are complete, and the report actually changes a decision. Do not count a favourable-looking result as value if the comparison has broken down.

LionLink should fund this proposal when an optional maintenance decision matters financially or operationally, genuine uncertainty remains, and a feasible trial can resolve enough of it. The next decision is approval of the intervention, eligible population, analysis plan, and resource commitment. If the available fleet is too small, records cannot support follow-up, or managers will change assignments to suit expectations, a software build will not repair the evidence. In that situation, improve measurement or use a more modest study with its limitations stated before promising a causal answer.
