---
title: Getting comparable workshop prices before choosing a supplier
description: A controlled price competition is useful only after suppliers accept the same work, quality requirements, and delivery terms.
---

A procurement officer receives several workshop proposals and wants to know which offers the best price. One quote covers diagnostic review, another covers cleaning, and a third includes component replacement. Choosing the smallest amount would not identify a cheaper way to buy the same work. It would select a different service. LionLink's [cost findings](/docs/cost-findings/) make this distinction explicit for the supplied fictional options. Their prices and validity periods describe proposals, not an interchangeable market or demonstrated savings.

A reverse auction could be useful after LionLink resolves that comparability problem. In a reverse auction, qualified suppliers compete to sell the same defined work by submitting lower prices during a published bidding period. The process would produce a reviewable award recommendation. It would not decide which maintenance intervention LionLink needs or treat the cheapest offer as acceptable regardless of quality. Those decisions must precede the price competition. The proposal is therefore conditional on a narrow service specification and enough willing qualified suppliers to make competition meaningful.

The fictional cost records contain scope, quoted price, visit counts, commercial terms, and validity dates. They do not establish supplier identities, qualifications, market interest, or agreement to a common statement of work. The [evidence sources](/docs/applications/) also distinguish quoted packages from approved purchases and incurred spending. Before building, procurement staff should test whether a real category of workshop work can be described consistently enough for suppliers to price it on the same basis. If each job requires substantial supplier-specific diagnosis, an auction may be the wrong procurement method.

For an illustrative suitable category, suppose Engineering has already approved a particular cleaning procedure and its inspection record. Procurement defines one lot, meaning one package of work offered for award. The specification states what the supplier must do, the required evidence of completion, the service window, and which costs the price includes. Suppliers first confirm that they can meet those terms and submit the qualification evidence required by LionLink's procurement process. Only accepted suppliers enter the bidding round. A low price cannot compensate for excluded mandatory work or a failed qualification.

During the round, each accepted bid receives a timestamped receipt. The service checks the supplier's eligibility, currency, completeness, and compliance with the announced bidding rules. For illustration, one qualified supplier may lower its offer before the deadline while an unqualified supplier submits an even smaller amount. The system accepts the eligible offer and rejects the ineligible one without placing it in the ranking. A bid that arrives after the published close also fails unless a closing-extension rule announced before the round applies. The same rule must apply to every participant, including suppliers favoured by a buyer.

The closing process needs a clear authority for time and an explicit tie rule. It must define whether suppliers can see only their rank or some other limited competitive information. Those choices influence participation and must be disclosed in advance. After closing, the system ranks the compliant final offers and records the history needed to explain the recommendation. A procurement officer then reviews the result under the approved award policy. The software should not create a purchase order automatically in the first implementation. Commercial approval remains a separate action based on the complete procurement record.

The reason to use this mechanism is price discovery for comparable work. A conventional request for quotations may already provide sufficient competition with less process and less software. An existing procurement platform may also support the required auction rules. A custom LionLink implementation makes sense only if recurring suitable purchases, integration needs, or evaluation requirements cannot be met economically through those alternatives. Supplier discovery comes first. A technically flawless auction with too few participants can add administrative cost while producing no useful competitive information.

A bounded prototype would use invented suppliers and one fixed lot. Procurement would define the qualification decision, permissible bid changes, deadline behaviour, tie handling, and award review. Engineering would own the statement of work and the evidence required at completion. Developers would implement supplier eligibility, bid submission and receipts, controlled visibility, closing, and an exportable award record. The prototype would not contact suppliers or create commercial commitments. A later live pilot would require supplier onboarding and integration with the approved procurement and purchase-order process, with policy review before launch.

The failure cases deserve as much attention as the ranking. If a supplier loses connectivity near closing, the announced rules must state whether any remedy exists. A server outage should trigger a documented suspension or restart process rather than an improvised extension offered to selected participants. Duplicate submissions need one clear accepted state and a receipt the supplier can use in a dispute. Bid information requires restricted access, and the event history should preserve corrections and administrative actions. Staff also need a way to cancel a round when the specification proves wrong without presenting the cancelled result as an award.

Quality remains an operational dependency after the auction. A supplier that prices aggressively may still fail to deliver the required work. Qualification and subsequent acceptance inspection must therefore remain meaningful. The auction does not prove delivery, establish that an invoice is correct, or forecast how many jobs LionLink should buy. Procurement staff should track supplier questions and withdrawn bids because they may reveal unclear terms or an unsuitable lot. Maintaining a reusable specification and supplier pool is ongoing work, not a one-time setup hidden outside the project cost.

Evaluation should first verify fairness and reproducibility with controlled cases. Tests should cover ineligible bids, late bids, tied prices, repeated submissions, and closing during a service interruption. An independent reviewer should be able to reconstruct the recommendation from accepted bids and the published rules. For a live pilot, compare like-for-like final terms, buyer effort, supplier participation, award completion, and delivered quality with the existing purchasing method. An apparent price reduction is not a saving if exclusions move cost elsewhere or poor delivery causes additional work. The current fictional quotes cannot supply that baseline.

The immediate recommendation is procurement discovery rather than a software build. LionLink should identify one recurring, standardized category and establish that qualified suppliers would participate under clear terms. If that evidence exists and an existing tool cannot meet the need, fund a limited auction capability with human award approval. If the work remains materially different between suppliers, improve the specification and ordinary quote comparison instead. The project earns its place through credible competition for equivalent work, not through a lower number on a screen.
