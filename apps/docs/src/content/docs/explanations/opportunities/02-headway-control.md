---
title: Keeping buses evenly spaced when service starts to bunch
description: How short, supervised departure holds could prevent an early spacing problem from becoming a long passenger wait.
---

A passenger waiting at a LionLink stop sees two buses arrive almost together after a long gap. One bus may be crowded while the next has spare seats. For the controller, the problem began earlier, when a delay changed the spacing between successive buses. Publishing a better timetable for tomorrow cannot correct that developing gap today. This proposal would give a controller a short-lived recommendation to hold a departure at an approved location when that action is likely to restore more even spacing without imposing excessive delay on passengers already aboard.

The [journey reliability findings](/docs/reliability-findings/) use fictional records of scheduled and actual departures and arrivals. The exercise also records passenger queues over selected windows. Those sources let the team examine timing and waiting within the supplied morning coverage. They do not provide live vehicle positions, prove that bunching is widespread, or establish that departure holding would improve LionLink's service. The implementation case depends on a narrower hypothesis that controllers face recoverable spacing disturbances and lack timely help deciding when a small intervention is better than leaving departures alone.

A fixed instruction to depart on time is useful, but it does not always address spacing after disruption. A bus delayed on its journey may collect passengers who accumulated during the longer gap ahead of it, while the following bus encounters fewer waiting passengers. Depending on boarding and traffic conditions, that difference can allow the following bus to catch up. This is a possible operating mechanism to test, not a conclusion demonstrated by the extract. Holding the following bus briefly could separate the pair, but a hold also delays people aboard and might enlarge the gap behind that bus. The decision therefore needs both neighboring vehicles.

The proposed method is feedback control, meaning that each recommendation uses the latest observed state and that subsequent observations determine the next recommendation. At a permitted departure point, the system estimates the time since the preceding bus and the expected time until the following bus. It compares those gaps with an agreed spacing objective. If a small hold would reduce a substantial imbalance, it calculates a recommendation within limits approved by operations. Small differences produce no action. That tolerance prevents controllers from receiving instructions for timing variations too minor to warrant interference.

For illustration, suppose a route aims for a ten-minute gap, the preceding bus left three minutes ago, and the next bus is expected seventeen minutes later. These numbers are invented to explain the decision, not taken from a LionLink service record. If operations permits a two-minute hold, the gaps would become five minutes ahead and fifteen minutes behind, assuming the following arrival does not change. The gaps remain uneven, but the correction stays within the approved bound. The screen would show the proposed hold, the latest observations, and its expiry time. The controller might reject it because the bus is carrying passengers with an important connection or because the expected following vehicle is unreliable. After any approved hold, the system observes the actual departure and recalculates rather than assuming the instruction had its intended effect.

A departure recommendation must expire because the situation changes while someone reads it. If the following bus is delayed unexpectedly, yesterday's logic applied even a few minutes late may now create a worse gap. The implementation must therefore distinguish a fresh recommendation from one awaiting new observations. Limits should cover both one hold and the total hold imposed on the same journey. Terminal departures, the last departures of the day, and services with particular connection commitments need explicit operating rules. The project cannot infer authority for live holding from the exercise's separate timetable-change allowances.

The first deliverable should be a replay tool for one route direction, not an automatic connection to drivers. It would process the historical trip events in time order and use a clearly marked synthetic stream for observations the extract lacks. Controllers could compare a no-hold policy with the proposed rule under the same disturbance scenarios. The replay should include isolated delays, successive disruptions, stale positions, and uncertainty about the next bus. It should reveal why a recommendation was produced and what passengers would experience under the model's assumptions. A simulation result would establish a candidate policy for further testing, not prove a real-world benefit.

Production requires reliable vehicle locations, matching between vehicles and trips, data timestamps, and an estimate of passenger loads. The operations team must identify safe holding locations and decide who may issue the instruction. Engineers must measure observation and delivery delay because a controller that reacts to old information can alternate between unnecessary corrections. An existing dispatch channel should carry any approved recommendation, with acknowledgement recorded separately. The proposed algorithm chooses a duration. A dispatch system remains responsible for delivering an authoritative instruction, and a human controller remains responsible for the operational decision during the pilot.

When the observations are incomplete or too old, the system should stop recommending holds and make that condition visible. It should not invent a vehicle position to keep the interface active. Controllers need a clear way to cancel a recommendation, record an exceptional condition, and resume the normal departure procedure. Adoption also requires restraint. Repeated low-value prompts would make a mathematically sound rule unusable in a busy control room. Review sessions should examine ignored recommendations and ask whether the missing context can be represented, whether the rule needs revision, or whether human judgement is sufficient at that location.

The value claim must include all affected passengers. More even gaps can reduce waiting under suitable arrival patterns, but that is not enough if the intervention causes greater delay aboard or missed connections. A pilot should compare spacing error, passenger waiting, onboard delay, and extreme waits across matched scenarios, then use supervised field observations if replay results justify them. The team should agree acceptable hold limits and passenger outcomes before tuning the controller. Evaluation must include realistic data latency and unfamiliar disturbances. A policy that looks good only with immediate, perfect observations has not earned live use.

LionLink should proceed only if it can identify a route and control point where recoverable spacing problems matter, a permitted hold is operationally useful, and observations arrive soon enough to support the decision. If the real cause is insufficient capacity or a timetable that cannot be operated, departure feedback will not solve it. The sponsor's next step is to authorize a bounded replay with controllers and agree the passenger trade-offs they would accept. A live pilot becomes reasonable when the rule improves those outcomes consistently and abstains safely when its information is inadequate.
