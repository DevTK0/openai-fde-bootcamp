---
title: Deciding which components deserve an earlier inspection
description: Why future failure estimates need complete component histories before they can improve on age-based maintenance decisions.
---

An engineer has time for an additional inspection and must choose which bus component to examine. A component with several recorded repairs may attract attention, but those repairs might span years of heavy use. Another component may have a shorter recorded history because it was installed recently. Counting repairs or sorting buses by age does not distinguish those situations. This proposal asks whether LionLink can estimate the chance of a component failing over a future period of use well enough to direct additional inspections, while leaving mandatory maintenance and reported defects under existing Engineering control.

The fictional maintenance evidence includes selected component observations, distance since previous component attention, monthly vehicle distance, and repair records. The [evidence sources](/docs/applications/) describe the eight-vehicle selection and warn that selected visits do not establish a complete history. The [repair spending explanation](/docs/maintenance-findings/) also distinguishes recorded cost and use from their possible causes. These records motivate a question about component risk, but they do not justify building a predictive maintenance model for the fleet. The immediate implementation case is for establishing whether the necessary histories can be reconstructed without misleading gaps.

A meaningful history starts with the component rather than only the bus. The team needs to know when a particular component entered service, how much it was used, what maintenance changed its condition, and whether it failed. Replacement starts a new history. An observation that the component was still working at the end of the dataset does not tell us its eventual lifetime. That incomplete lifetime still contains useful information: the component survived at least until the last observation. A method that treats every such component as having failed at the cutoff would overstate risk, while dropping them would ignore evidence from components that lasted.

Survival analysis is the family of methods designed for that distinction. It estimates the chance of an event over time or exposure while retaining histories that end before the event occurs. Those incomplete histories are called censored observations. For LionLink, exposure might be distance since a verified installation, provided distance is reliable and appropriate for the component. The result would be an estimated probability of failure before a proposed future exposure, with an uncertainty range. It is not a predicted exact failure date, and the interface should not present it as one.

For illustration, consider two otherwise comparable components. One has a verified installation and a known distance in service. The other has a condition inspection but no reliable installation date. The first might support an estimate if there are enough comparable complete histories. The second should remain unscored or carry an explicit statement that its age is unknown. Giving both components precise-looking rankings would create confidence unsupported by their records. If the first component's estimated risk range remains wide, the engineer may reasonably keep the ordinary age-based inspection order. An honest abstention can be a correct result of the system.

A positive result also needs a concrete decision. Suppose two components have usable histories and the validated estimator suggests a higher failure risk for component A than component B over the same next 5,000 kilometres. This is a hypothetical example, not a measured LionLink result. If the uncertainty and operating consequences satisfy Engineering's agreed criteria, an engineer assigns the available additional inspection to A and records the observed condition. That finding can confirm whether the inspection was useful, without claiming that an unobserved future failure was prevented.

The proposed decision concerns additional inspection, not permission to postpone required work. A reported defect or a mandatory interval must retain its existing priority regardless of a low model estimate. After an engineer chooses an earlier inspection, the resulting finding can help evaluate whether the estimate was useful. It does not automatically prove that a failure was prevented. The distinction matters because successful intervention changes what happens next. A component removed before failure no longer provides an observed natural failure time, and its removal may reflect suspicion of deterioration rather than a routine cutoff unrelated to condition.

That last issue creates a substantial data requirement. The team must record why observation ended, including preventive replacement, transfer, missing records, and confirmed failure. Even complete fields may leave a biased comparison if engineers remove the most suspect components before failure and the reasons are not captured well enough to account for that selection. It needs stable component identifiers, installation and replacement dates, exposure, and consistent definitions of failure by component family. A bus-level repair count cannot substitute for these facts. Engineering, the maintenance-system owner, and a data analyst should inspect a sample of real work orders together and decide whether a reliable component episode can be created. If they cannot agree where histories begin and end, collecting more rows will not make the model trustworthy.

The first funded deliverable should therefore be a data feasibility study for one component family. It should produce a reviewed set of component histories, explain excluded or uncertain histories, and demonstrate the intended calculations on synthetic cases where the underlying lifetimes are known. The supplied observations can illustrate the expected fields and their gaps. They cannot establish predictive accuracy. Only if the feasibility study finds sufficient reliable failures and recorded use of components that have not failed should the team compare a simple survival estimator with the current age-based approach. Starting with a complicated model would make the data problems harder to identify.

Evaluation must reserve vehicles or component histories that were not used to fit the model. Repeated observations of one component cannot count as independent proof that it generalizes to another. The team should check calibration, meaning whether events occur about as often as predicted among comparable risk estimates. It should also examine the actual inspection decision: unnecessary inspections consume workshop capacity, while missed developing faults can leave the original operating problem unresolved. Those outcomes should be reported separately, with uncertainty, rather than collapsed into one accuracy percentage that ignores their different consequences.

A production pilot would require Engineering to define when an estimate is strong enough to influence an additional inspection and when the system must decline to rank a component. The maintenance interface should show the relevant history and the uncertainty behind the suggestion. A reviewer must be able to correct a component identity or installation date and withdraw the resulting recommendation. Ongoing work includes auditing failure capture, monitoring changes in component design or operating conditions, and reassessing calibration. A model trained on one selected group should not silently extend to a different component family or fleet population.

The sponsor should not authorize a fleet-wide prediction product from the current exercise. The defensible next investment is a focused history reconstruction with Engineering, followed by a simple comparison only if the data passes that review. If records remain incomplete, failure events are too scarce, or age-based rules perform just as well, LionLink should improve data capture and keep the simpler policy. This proposal earns an implementation beyond that study only when its estimates change inspection priorities in a measurable, reviewable way without weakening existing maintenance obligations.
