---
title: Warning passengers before they miss an unfamiliar stop
description: A device can follow progress along a selected route and give a destination alert while keeping location samples local.
---

A passenger on an unfamiliar LionLink route wants to read or rest without repeatedly checking every stop. A destination warning could help, but a naive alert based only on distance can mislead them. On a loop route, the vehicle may pass close to the destination while travelling along the wrong section. Tall buildings or a weak location signal may also place the phone near a stop the bus has not reached. The useful product is a warning based on credible journey progress, with an honest indication when the phone cannot determine that progress.

The proposal would let the passenger choose the route, direction, and destination, then follow the journey on the device. Recent position samples would remain local rather than become a server-side history of the passenger's movements. The device would warn when it recognizes a plausible approach to the selected stop. This is an assistance hypothesis, not evidence that LionLink passengers frequently miss stops. The supplied passenger accounts do not establish demand for location-based alerts, so early research should identify who needs the cue and what form of warning they can use.

LionLink's fictional network records contain stop coordinates and an ordered sequence of stops on each route. The order is important because a route can revisit a location. Historical trip identifiers can describe a journey but do not provide the phone's current position or a live service feed. The [evidence sources](/docs/applications/) explain those boundaries, and the [reliability findings](/docs/reliability-findings/) show why schedules and observed timing have different meanings. A prototype must obtain device location samples and their reported accuracy separately. It must also establish whether the selected platform supports reliable alerts while the phone is locked.

The mechanism keeps track of a small number of journey states. At first, the journey is unconfirmed because the passenger has selected a route but the phone has not established that they are travelling along it. Once recent positions show plausible progress through the ordered route, tracking begins. An approach to the target makes an alert eligible. After the warning fires, the journey enters an alerted state so that small location movements do not repeat it. Engineers call this a state machine. It is a set of explicit rules for moving between these situations rather than a guess based on a single location sample.

Consider an illustrative loop route that passes near the destination on its outward leg and reaches the boarding stop later on its return. The passenger selects the return-leg stop. A location sample close to that stop on the outward leg should not trigger the warning because the recognized stop sequence has not reached the relevant part of the journey. Later, several consistent samples show progress toward the correct occurrence. The device issues the chosen audio, vibration, or visual cue. If location noise then moves the estimated position outside and inside the alert boundary, the alerted state prevents repeated warnings.

The device also needs different rules for entering and leaving an approach area. Otherwise, a noisy estimate near one threshold can repeatedly change the displayed status. A gap in the signal should reduce confidence instead of allowing the system to invent smooth progress. When the gap is too large, the passenger should see that the alert cannot currently be relied on, together with the ordered stop list. Correcting the direction or ending the journey must be straightforward. The system cannot assume that a passenger who selected a route actually boarded that service.

A static stop list, an onboard announcement, or an alarm set for an approximate journey time may already meet the need for many passengers. Custom sensing is worthwhile only if users benefit from a cue tied to their actual progress and supported devices can deliver it dependably. This proposal does not choose an accessible path or confirm that a passenger can complete a transfer. It follows a journey the passenger has already chosen. That narrower purpose keeps the first implementation focused on noisy sensing, route order, and device behaviour rather than trying to become another general journey planner.

The first deliverable should support one route with a loop or repeated nearby stops and one clearly stated device platform. Engineers would download the relevant stop sequence, implement local progress recognition, and expose permission and uncertainty states. Product staff would work with intended passengers to choose the cue and the desired warning interval. Network data owners would confirm route direction and stop ordering. The application would need location and notification permission, and its selected platform would determine whether a browser implementation is sufficient or a native capability is necessary. A successful demonstration in an open browser does not answer that platform question.

Failure recovery should prioritize clarity. If notifications are disabled, the interface must say which supported cue remains available before the journey starts. If background execution stops, the product must not continue claiming that protection is active. Battery use should be measured on the supported device because continuous sensing can make an otherwise helpful feature unacceptable. Location samples should be discarded after they serve the current journey unless the passenger explicitly chooses a separate diagnostic process. A support report can often describe the selected route and failure state without uploading a detailed movement history.

Adoption would start with accompanied trials rather than asking passengers to depend on an unproven alert. Participants should retain the ordinary stop announcements and route information. The team should observe whether warnings arrive early enough to prepare, whether they interrupt too often, and whether uncertainty messages are understood. Accessibility testing matters because a vibration-only cue or an audio-only cue will not suit every passenger. The wording must describe assistance rather than a guarantee that the passenger will alight safely. Ongoing support includes checking location and notification behaviour after operating-system updates and maintaining route sequences when services change.

Verification should replay invented position traces for a correct approach, a nearby wrong leg, repeated noisy boundary crossings, and a prolonged gap. Those controlled traces establish whether the rules behave as intended. Real-device journeys then establish whether the phone supplies useful data and delivers the notification under supported conditions, including a locked screen. Measure missed warnings, warnings on the wrong leg, lead time, battery cost, and passenger confidence in relation to actual performance. A low average timing error can conceal the occasional missed cue that matters most to the intended user.

The next investment should be a device feasibility test and passenger observation on one route. If the supported platform cannot provide a dependable background cue, LionLink should either change the platform scope or stop the proposal before polishing the interface. If passengers find the alert useful and trials establish acceptable failure behaviour, a narrow release is reasonable. Its justification is reduced attention required during an unfamiliar journey, supported by measured device performance rather than the mere availability of stop coordinates.
