---
title: Letting controllers practise difficult decisions before a live incident
description: Branching exercises can teach evidence gathering and escalation through a replay of the trainee's own choices.
---

A controller receives an update that a vehicle is expected to finish workshop work soon. A relief service needs cover, and the vehicle appears to be the nearest answer. The difficult part is recognizing what the update does not establish. Expected completion is not confirmed Engineering release, and a takeover briefing does not itself authorize dispatch. A controller who can repeat those rules in a classroom may still need practice applying them while requests arrive in an inconvenient order.

LionLink could provide that practice through short interactive incident exercises. Each exercise would reveal information over time, ask the trainee to choose an action or request evidence, and explain the consequences afterward. The purpose would be to improve procedural judgment before a person has to exercise it in a live service. This is a training hypothesis rather than evidence of poor controller performance. Training owners should identify a particular decision that current instruction does not teach well before commissioning the software.

The fictional incident baseline supplies suitable ingredients. It includes an incident time, takeover rules, protected duties, and relief-demand assumptions. The [workshop findings](/docs/workshop-findings/) explain the distinction between expected completion and confirmed release. Timestamped operational resource updates offer another way to construct information that arrives in stages. As the [evidence sources](/docs/applications/) make clear, these records are exercise material. They can support a credible fictional scenario, but they cannot establish the competence of real staff or the frequency of a particular mistake.

The proposed mechanism is an instructor-authored set of branching decisions. A branch is a point at which different choices lead to different prepared next steps. Engineers can represent the exercise as connected scenes, with each connection corresponding to an action. The trainee sees only the information available in the current scene. Requesting release confirmation might reveal a new message after some scenario time. Dispatching immediately might lead to a consequence that the instructor has written to expose the missed check. Escalating may lead to a different, defensible continuation. The exercise need not pretend that there is always one perfect answer.

Consider an illustrative rehearsal built around the vehicle awaiting release. The trainee receives an estimated completion time and a request for cover. They can seek confirmation, escalate the shortage, or issue a dispatch instruction. If they seek confirmation, the next scene states that Engineering has not yet released the vehicle. The trainee must then decide how to handle the unmet service need using the approved procedure. If they dispatch early, the debrief identifies the evidence gap at the moment of the decision. It should not criticize the trainee using facts that appeared only later in the exercise.

That replay is the main learning product. It places each choice beside the information the trainee had, the relevant procedure, and the instructor's explanation. A request for missing information can receive credit even if the eventual outcome remains inconvenient. A reasonable escalation should not be marked wrong merely because an alternative route leads to a happier scripted ending. The scoring rules should separate procedural errors from choices that are acceptable under uncertainty. Where experienced instructors disagree, the scenario needs clarification before the score can be trusted.

A tabletop exercise can provide excellent discussion and may be the better option for a small team. Its limitation is the facilitator time required for repeated individual practice and consistent replay. Software is justified if LionLink needs trainees to practise the same decision pattern independently, encounter different information orders, and review their own reasoning with an instructor afterward. It should not attempt to replace instructor judgment with a numerical score. Nor should it estimate queue lengths or real operational outcomes unless a separately validated simulation supports those claims. An authored consequence is an instructional device, not a forecast.

The first deliverable should be one short exercise with an instructor-approved procedure and a small number of meaningful branches. Training staff would write the scenario, identify what each choice demonstrates, and review the debrief language. Experienced controllers would check whether the available facts support the choices. Engineers would implement timed information release, action recording, deterministic replay, and versioned scenario content. Deterministic replay means that the same scenario and recorded choices produce the same explanation, allowing an instructor to inspect what the trainee actually saw. The exercise can run entirely on fictional records without integration to live dispatch systems.

Scenario maintenance needs explicit ownership. When a procedure changes, the training owner must review every affected branch and retire outdated versions. A learner's old result should remain tied to the scenario they completed rather than acquire a new interpretation silently. The initial system should provide private practice feedback and an instructor discussion record. Using results for employment or certification decisions would require separate evidence that the exercise measures the intended skill fairly. A polished interface and a precise score do not provide that evidence.

The design also needs to resist memorization. Repeating identical buttons in identical order can teach the answer sequence instead of the reasoning. Instructors can vary when a relevant update arrives, alter a nonessential detail, or ask the trainee to explain which fact permits the next action. Those variations should remain reviewed and bounded. Automatically generated incidents could introduce ambiguities or teach an incorrect procedure. If a trainee reports a missing option, the system should capture the concern for instructor review rather than force a choice that misrepresents the person's judgment.

Evaluation should involve both experienced controllers and newer learners. Experienced controllers can identify unrealistic constraints and disputed scoring. Learners can show whether the debrief explains their mistake in terms they can apply to a different scenario. A useful pilot measures instructor preparation and review effort, completion problems, and performance on a separate unfamiliar exercise. Improvement on the same memorized scenario is weak evidence of learning. The team should also check that future facts never leak into the current scene and that interrupted sessions resume without changing the available information.

The case for implementation rests on repeated practice that instructors cannot economically provide through existing exercises. LionLink should fund the first scenario and working replay together only after a training owner names the target skill and agrees how transfer of learning will be assessed. If experts cannot agree on the correct procedure, the immediate work is procedural clarification. If a facilitated discussion already meets the training need, custom software adds little. A successful first exercise would justify expanding the library one validated decision pattern at a time, with instructor maintenance included in the cost.
