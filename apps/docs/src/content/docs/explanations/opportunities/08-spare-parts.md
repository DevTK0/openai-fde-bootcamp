---
title: Ordering spare parts before a repair has to wait for delivery
description: How a replenishment policy could balance parts availability against excess stock, using real consumption and supplier delivery records.
---

A mechanic confirms a repair, but the required part is not in stock. The bus remains unavailable while the parts team finds a supplier and waits for delivery. Buying a large quantity of every possible part would avoid some shortages, but would also consume money and storage, with some items becoming obsolete before use. This proposal concerns the recurring decision between those extremes: when to replenish a particular part and how much to order, given uncertain future demand and delivery time.

The LionLink exercise records repair actions, combined labour and parts charges, repair counts, and vehicle unavailability. The [repair spending findings](/docs/maintenance-findings/) explain how recorded costs relate to vehicle use and where comparisons stop. Those records justify investigating the causes of downtime and spending. They do not show which parts were consumed, whether any repair waited for stock, or what proportion of downtime a replenishment policy could prevent. The business case must begin by distinguishing a parts shortage from other causes of delay. A repair-count chart cannot provide that distinction.

A practical current process might use a storekeeper's experience and fixed minimum quantities. That can work well for a small, stable range of parts. The proposed system adds value when consumption, supplier reliability, or the fitted fleet changes enough that those fixed levels become difficult to maintain. Its central decision is a stock policy, not a prediction of the next individual vehicle fault. Even if the team cannot know which bus needs a part next week, it may be able to hold an appropriate quantity for the combined demand over the time needed to replenish it.

The method starts with an accurate stock position. The system counts usable items on hand, adds confirmed incoming orders, and subtracts quantities already committed to work. It then compares that position with a reorder threshold based on expected demand during supplier delivery time and an agreed allowance for uncertainty. When the position crosses the threshold, the system proposes an order that restores an agreed target level. The parts coordinator reviews the proposal against storage, supplier minimums, shelf life, and approved substitutions. Purchasing authority remains with the existing process.

For an illustrative calculation, suppose there are four usable items on the shelf, two committed to repairs, and three on a confirmed order. The stock position is five. If the approved reorder threshold is six and the target is ten, the system proposes five additional items, subject to supplier constraints. These numbers explain the arithmetic and are not LionLink inventory observations. Ignoring the existing order would propose too much stock. Ignoring committed parts would make the shelf look healthier than it is. The proposal should show those quantities and the timestamp of the inventory snapshot so the coordinator can verify the calculation.

Setting the threshold is the harder part. The team needs to understand demand while waiting for a delivery, including the possibility of a late delivery. If the part is common to many buses, a cluster of similar failures might create more demand than an ordinary period. The coordinator and Engineering must agree how serious a shortage would be and whether an approved substitute exists. A highly consequential, non-substitutable part may justify a different policy from a routine consumable. No statistical calculation can decide that operating preference without an accountable owner.

The first useful implementation should be a replay for one part with a complete transaction history. It should reconstruct receipts, issues, returns, commitments, and outstanding orders, then compare the existing fixed rule with a proposed replenishment policy over the same demand sequence. The fixture lacks that ledger, so an initial synthetic ledger must be clearly identified as a demonstration rather than business evidence. A production pilot should wait until the storekeeper can reconcile the real opening and closing balances. If the ledger cannot explain how many items exist, changing the reorder formula is premature.

Required integrations include the stock system, purchase orders, goods receipts, and work-order part issues. Engineering must confirm part compatibility and acceptable alternatives. Similar descriptions must not cause the system to merge different part numbers. Supplier delivery history should distinguish promised dates from actual receipts, and partial deliveries should remain visible. Demand also needs to include requests that could not be fulfilled. If an unavailable part never receives an issue transaction, learning demand only from issued stock would make the system underestimate the very shortage it is supposed to prevent.

The policy should create a reviewable purchase proposal rather than place an order automatically during the first release. If stock changes before approval, the proposal must be recalculated against the new position. A stable proposal identity and reference to the existing open orders can prevent duplicate replenishment after a retry. If an integration is unavailable, the coordinator should see which quantities are stale and return to the established manual process. The system must not quietly interpret a failed stock read as zero inventory. That error could turn a connection problem into an unnecessary purchase.

A parts coordinator should own regular policy review, with Engineering involved when the fleet or component specification changes. Operational adoption means recording issues and returns at the time they occur rather than repairing the ledger later. That work has a cost and belongs in the investment decision. The pilot should measure unfilled requests, time repairs wait specifically for the chosen part, stock held, expired or obsolete items, and coordinator effort. It should also test delayed deliveries and clustered demand. A policy that reduces shortages only by holding far more inventory than the operator accepts has not solved the stated problem.

LionLink should first commission a stock and demand audit for a part that staff believe contributes to repair delays. If reconciled records confirm recurring shortages and there is freedom to change order timing or quantity, a replenishment pilot has a clear purpose. If supplier minimum quantities dominate every decision, or poor issue recording explains the apparent shortages, software for finer thresholds will contribute little. The next sponsor decision is to choose one part with the storekeeper, verify its transaction history, and agree what availability is worth relative to stock held. Only then can a tested policy justify broader implementation.
