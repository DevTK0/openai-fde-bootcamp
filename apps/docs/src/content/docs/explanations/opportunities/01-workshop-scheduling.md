---
title: Scheduling workshop repairs without cancelling protected services
description: Why LionLink could use a scheduling tool to fit maintenance, staff, replacement vehicles, and passenger duties into one workable plan.
---

A LionLink workshop planner has two reasonable requests for the same morning. Each vehicle needs maintenance, each job has a completion deadline, and passenger services still need buses and drivers. The plan must preserve the trips designated as protected, meaning that the planner cannot cancel them to make maintenance fit. Approving the requests separately can create a promise the workshop cannot keep. A delayed repair then becomes an operating problem when the bus is due back in service. The proposed project is a tool that prepares a workable maintenance schedule and its service-cover arrangements together, so the planner can approve the whole commitment rather than discover a conflict after work starts.

The exercise already contains a concrete example. The [workshop findings](/docs/workshop-findings/) describe two requests starting at 09:00 on 19 October 2026. Both require a bay, and together they require three technicians. The available workshop has one bay and two technicians at a time. These fictional records prove that the proposed starts conflict. They do not prove that LionLink regularly loses services because of workshop scheduling, or that software would save a particular amount of money. The first business question is whether comparable conflicts occur often enough, and involve enough interacting commitments, to justify help beyond a planner's diary.

Moving the second job later appears to solve the bay problem. It may also make the second bus unavailable for a protected passenger trip, push the work past its deadline, or require a replacement driver after that driver's availability ends. The supplied planning records offer only one additional cover vehicle. A spare bus cannot cover two duties at once, and its presence alone says nothing about a suitable crew. This is why the proposal treats the workshop and service plan as one decision. A board that highlights overlapping appointments helps someone notice the issue, but the planner still has to discover a feasible replacement arrangement.

The tool would represent every job as a block of time with stated resource needs. It would then search combinations of start times, qualified staff, and eligible cover assignments. Rules such as one job per bay, no overlapping crew commitments, and preservation of protected trips would exclude invalid combinations. This method is called constraint scheduling. The important property for a planner is that a proposed schedule must satisfy every mandatory rule before the tool compares convenience or idle time. It must never decide that a missed protected trip is an acceptable price for a tidier workshop calendar.

Consider an illustrative planning conversation using the supplied start times. The planner asks whether one job can run first and the other follow it. The tool checks the first job's duration, then carries the second vehicle's absence through its passenger duties. If the cover bus is already needed elsewhere during that absence, the proposal fails even though the bay is free. The tool identifies the overlapping cover requirement and shows which input established it. The planner can then consider an earlier start, another approved cover resource, or a different maintenance day. None is assumed to be available. If the planner changes an input, the tool checks the entire proposal again. In a hypothetical successful branch, operations confirms another eligible cover bus and driver for the conflicting duty. The new proposal places the jobs in sequence, names both cover assignments, and confirms their complete returns fit the available windows. That is an example of the accepted output, not a claim that the additional resources exist in the fixture.

This explanation is part of the deliverable. A rejected request should identify the conflict in operational language, such as a named cover vehicle being committed when a protected trip needs it. A successful proposal should show job times, the associated cover duties, and the assumptions that must remain true. The planner needs to understand why the plan works before accepting it. A tool that only displays a score or announces that no solution exists would leave most of the difficult work with the user. Some conflicts require several requirements together, so the explanation must not invent a single cause when the implementation cannot establish one.

The first implementation should handle the two supplied requests, one workshop window, and their named service obligations. It should produce a proposal for review without creating bookings in a live system. A planner and an operations controller would check the same proposal independently against the source records. The prototype should also include a deliberately shortened cover window, so the team can see that the system refuses an impossible arrangement rather than quietly removing a trip. This bounded exercise establishes whether the scheduling method and its explanations are useful before the project expands to an entire depot.

Real use needs more than the exercise extract. The workshop manager must supply current bookings, technician skills, equipment requirements, and credible job durations. Operations must supply complete duties, positioning travel, takeover time, and cover availability. Engineering must define how a vehicle becomes eligible for service after maintenance. A proposed completion time is not an Engineering release. These responsibilities need named owners because an accurate calculation over stale information can still produce an unusable plan. Integration should start with a reviewed import or read-only connection, followed later by approved booking writes if the pilot earns that step.

Repair duration is another limitation. An apparently feasible plan may have no room for a job to overrun. The planner should agree how much allowance a job needs and see which service commitment would become threatened first. If a repair runs late, the tool should stop presenting the old plan as current and prepare alternatives from the remaining available resources. The controller retains authority over service changes, and the workshop retains its normal release procedure. If data connections fail, the last proposal remains a dated planning record, not permission to carry out assignments that can no longer be checked.

The operational benefit would come from finding feasible combinations sooner and catching hidden commitments before approval. A pilot should compare the tool with the existing manual process on the same requests. Relevant outcomes include time spent preparing and revising a plan, conflicts found before approval, and plans later abandoned because an input was wrong. Every proposed schedule also needs an independent resource and service check. Faster planning is not valuable if it produces fragile arrangements or moves extra reconciliation work to controllers. The workshop planning owner should maintain scheduling rules with operations approval for service constraints. Ongoing work also includes correcting resource data and reviewing cases where planners reject a technically feasible suggestion.

LionLink should fund this as a narrow planning pilot if workshop and operations staff confirm that coupled scheduling conflicts are a recurring burden and can provide authoritative inputs. If a shared calendar and a clearer cover approval step solve the observed problem, those changes are the better investment. The next sponsor decision is therefore to nominate one workshop planner and one controller to replay representative requests and compare both approaches. Expansion would be justified by usable explanations and fewer planning failures under realistic constraints, not by the existence of an optimizer or an attractive calendar.
